5 Things Nobody Tells You About Being the Walk-Up Song Parent

By Andrew Testa · Chief Hype Officer, and the guy who drags the speaker
Published October 19, 2026

There’s no training when you sign up to run the walk-up songs.

What starts out as you innocently wanting to play walk-up songs turns into you being responsible for ALL the vibes for every game.

Here are five things I wish I knew going in.

1Your role is bigger than you think

The right song or announcement can wake up the dugout, get players smiling and make a normal Saturday game feel a little bigger. It can also get you into trouble with the umpire, opposing first base coach — or even your own team’s coach if you’re not careful.

This should probably require adult supervision.

Thankfully it doesn’t.

2The game moves crazy fast

Especially when everyone isn’t getting walked around the bases.

Some on-deck circles are basically three steps from home plate. You need to know who’s next, have the song ready and stay locked in.

And between innings, once you hear “Balls in! Coming down!”, your window is officially closing.

This is not the job for scrolling TikTok between batters.

3Your job is not always appreciated

Some people think walk-up songs are a stain on today’s game.

Coaches, umpires and tournament ops won’t always love everything you bring to the table. Volume, song choices and sound effects can all become a discussion very quickly.

It can also get pretty awkward when you’re getting mercy ruled or you’re the one handing out the blowout.

Frankly, that’s their problem.

But it’s still a good idea to keep relations friendly. At least most of the time.

4It’s a commitment. It doesn’t need to become a second job.

Showing up early, dragging the speaker across wet grass and staying locked in to every at-bat?

That comes with the territory.

Chasing parents for songs, dealing with bad Wi-Fi at the field, paying for announcements and fighting with your setup shouldn’t.

5You don’t expect how good it feels when players and parents tell you it matters

Travel baseball and softball become little families.

The songs, introductions, dugout jokes and game-day traditions start becoming part of the season itself.

And when a player or parent tells you how much they enjoy it, you realize you’re helping create a small part of the experience they’re going to remember.

That feels pretty awesome.

What did I miss?

Every Speaker Parent learns something the hard way.

If there’s one thing nobody warned you about before you took over the walk-up songs, come tell me — I’m collecting them over on Instagram.
